Why processional routes matter to the way we read cities

A route is more than a line

Urban analysis often treats movement as a problem of efficiency: where people begin, where they go and how quickly they arrive. Processional routes complicate this view. They carry accumulated meanings, recurring rituals and social agreements that become visible through movement.

In post-colonial cities, these routes can also reveal the persistence of inherited spatial structures. Street widths, intersections, thresholds and centralities shape what a procession can do—but collective practice can also temporarily rewrite those spatial conditions.

Reading spatial and cultural evidence together

My research combines historical mapping, spatial network analysis and observation of collective movement. None of these sources is sufficient alone. The historical map records one version of the city; syntax measures describe relational properties; the event reveals how space is actually negotiated.

The methodological question is therefore not simply “where did the route go?” It is “what spatial knowledge does the route produce?”

Toward reusable urban evidence

Documenting the steps from archival source to mapped route matters. A transparent workflow allows evidence to be checked, extended and compared across cities. This is one reason the datasets and computational methods associated with this research will be documented openly as they mature.
