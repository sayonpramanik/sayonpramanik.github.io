Teaching the city: from spatial organisation to spatial intelligibility

How do we explain a city to someone who has only encountered parts of it? A plan shows streets and buildings at once. A person walking through them sees a sequence: an opening ahead, a turn, a landmark, perhaps a route whose continuation is unclear. The distance between those two views has long interested me as both a teacher of architecture and a researcher of urban space.

At NIT Raipur, my teaching included Theory of Design and Human Settlement and Spatial Organization. An institutional elective list records my association with both subjects. They invite related but distinct questions. How is a spatial arrangement made? And how do people recognise, use and learn that arrangement over time? My current research brings those questions together through the study of urban form and collective movement.

The city as a pedagogical problem

Architecture teaching often begins with a representation: a drawing, model or map that lets us see relationships we could not see from a single position on the ground. This is useful, but it can also make the city appear more legible than it is in use. On a drawing, the path through a square may be obvious. At street level, a boundary, change of level, crowd or missing sightline can alter what the same path offers.

A good teaching question is therefore not only whether a plan is formally coherent. It is whether someone can orient themselves within it, reach the places they need, and understand where they are in relation to a larger system. The answer may differ for a visitor, a resident, a group travelling together or a person whose mobility is constrained. No single drawing can settle it.

From organisation to intelligibility

Spatial organisation describes how places are arranged and connected. Spatial intelligibility asks a further question: what can someone infer about that arrangement from the portion they can encounter? A connected network does not guarantee that every route is easy to read. Equally, a familiar route may remain meaningful even when a plan suggests a more direct alternative.

Space Syntax provides one way to study relational properties of a layout. It can help identify patterns of connection, depth and potential movement. These are analytical descriptions of space, not direct measurements of what any particular person knows or chooses. To understand experience, we also need to attend to visibility, landmarks, prior knowledge, social practice and the circumstances in which a journey takes place.

This distinction matters to my research because I am interested in more than how a street network permits movement. I want to know how spatial relationships become readable, how people act on them, and how repeated use changes the knowledge they bring to the next journey.

Movement as a way of knowing

Walking is often treated as an outcome of spatial form: a person chooses a route through a given network. It is also a means of learning that network. Each passage can confirm an expectation, reveal a shortcut, make a threshold familiar or change the significance of a place. What is visible from a route and what is remembered from earlier visits both shape the next decision.

Collective movement adds another layer. A procession, for example, may follow a route whose importance cannot be explained by shortest-path logic. Its timing, participants and remembered associations can temporarily change how a street is occupied and understood. This is why I read processional routes alongside historical evidence and spatial configuration, rather than treating the geometry of a route as its complete explanation.

What computation can add

Different methods help ask different questions. Space Syntax can describe configurational relationships; GIS can bring historical maps and contemporary networks into a comparable spatial frame; observation can document how people actually move and gather. Computational tools can make it easier to compare these registers across places and times. They cannot remove the need to ask how each source was produced and what it leaves out.

Historical maps, for instance, may omit the everyday practices that gave a route its meaning. Movement traces can show where people went without revealing why. A measure of network centrality may indicate an opportunity for movement without showing whether a particular group could use that route comfortably or safely. These are reasons to combine methods carefully, not reasons to abandon measurement.

The research question is not whether a model can reproduce the whole experience of a city. It cannot. A more productive question is where measured configuration, observed movement and accounts of lived or historical use support one another—and where they disagree. Those disagreements can tell us what to investigate next.

Returning to the studio

This line of inquiry also returns to teaching. A spatial analysis can make an assumption in a design proposal visible: perhaps a route is well connected on paper but hard to recognise from the ground, or an apparent shortcut crosses a place people use for other purposes. Bringing such evidence into a studio discussion does not turn design into an exercise in optimising a score. It gives students a better question to work with.

For me, the connection between teaching and research lies there. Teaching asks us to explain why a spatial decision matters. Research asks how we might test that explanation against the city as people encounter it. Both require moving between a view of the whole and the partial, changing knowledge available to someone within it.
