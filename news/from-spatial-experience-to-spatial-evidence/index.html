From spatial experience to spatial evidence

Cities are experienced before they are measured. A street can feel inviting, a junction confusing, or a route familiar before any of these experiences becomes a variable in an analytical model. For research on urban form and collective movement, the question is how to bring those experiences into a conversation with spatial evidence.

Bringing different kinds of evidence together

Space Syntax offers a way to examine relationships within a spatial configuration. Movement observations register how people occupy and negotiate that configuration. Historical maps help trace changes in the streets and places through which movement occurs. Each provides a different view; none can stand in for all the others.

In studying processional routes, this distinction matters. A connected street network may help explain the possibilities for movement, but the meaning of a particular route also depends on cultural practice, memory and the circumstances of an event. A mapped line needs to remain connected to the sources and observations that support it.

Keeping interpretation visible

More detailed spatial data can make patterns easier to examine. It does not, by itself, explain why those patterns matter. The useful methodological task is to ask where configuration, movement and historical evidence agree, where they diverge, and what further observation would help resolve the difference.

This is a research reflection rather than a report of completed empirical findings. It sets out a question that connects my work on urban form, collective movement and spatial culture: how can different forms of spatial evidence inform one another while retaining their context and limitations?
